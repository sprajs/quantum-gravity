# The one-particle cost of a sharp spherical rim

In the free Minkowski graviton vacuum, a spherical quadrupole with nonzero optical slope at the rim gives a logarithmically divergent one-particle norm squared for the linearized clock response. Each successive octave of master frequency adds the same limiting weight. A reference wave whose optical slope vanishes at the rim has finite local ultraviolet weight there. This tests the mixed Hessian of the matched-radius clock on the flat background, using the ordinary Einstein symplectic form and one specified representation. The nonlinear clock operator and its spectrum remain open. [Chandrasekaran, arXiv:2611.34924v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34924v1) · [Spherical clock Hessian](joint-flat-hessian.md) · [Weak master inverse](weak-master.md)

## The Minkowski norm

Take the waist radius as the length unit, the initial rim at $0<a<1$, and the radial quadrupole generated by $f(s)=F(2s)$ with the tensor $S=\operatorname{diag}(-1/2,-1/2,1)$. Write $G_W$ for the coefficient of the trace-free conformal screen strain of a smooth reference wave $W$. The ADM symplectic form for real smooth compactly supported full-line master profiles is
$$
 \Omega(f,g)=A\int_{\mathbb R} f^{(9)}g\,ds,\qquad A=\frac{3}{5120G_N}.
$$
Time translations act by translations of the master argument in $F(t+r)-F(t-r)$. A profile $e^{-iks}$ therefore has Minkowski time dependence $e^{-ikt/2}$. The real positive-frequency complex structure $J$ acts in the master Fourier transform by multiplication by $i\operatorname{sgn}k$ up to the orientation sign. For $\widehat f(k)=\int e^{-iks}f(s)ds$, Parseval's formula gives the real one-particle quadratic form
$$
 \mu(f)=|\Omega(f,Jf)|=\frac{A}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}|k|^9|\widehat f(k)|^2dk
       =\frac{A}{\pi}\int_0^\infty k^9|\widehat f(k)|^2dk . \tag{1}
$$
This choice fixes the normalization of the reported norm. The wavenumber $k$ is conjugate to $s$. A cutoff on Minkowski time frequency uses $k/2$. [Cartesian ADM normalization](weak-normalization.md)

## The local test

The weak first-slot equation determines $f_X$ on an interval about $a$ up to a polynomial of degree at most eight. The solution is smooth on either open side. Its value and first three derivatives agree across the rim, and its fourth derivative has right-minus-left jump
$$
 d=-16G_N(1-a)G'_W(a).
$$
For example, write $L g=G'_g$, whose highest derivative is $g^{(5)}/(16s)$. The mixed Hessian on a compactly supported test is $(3/20)\int_a^1 s(1-s)G'_W Lg\,ds$. The leading boundary term after integration by parts is $-(3/320)(1-a)G'_W(a)g^{(4)}(a)$. In $A f_X^{(9)}$ it comes from $Ad\,\delta_a^{(4)}$. All lower terms have smaller distributional order. [Distributional inverse and its normalization](weak-master.md)

Take a real $\psi\in C_c^\infty$ supported in this interval, equal to one near $a$, and set $u=\psi f_X$. Its fifth derivative is a point mass $d\,\delta_a$ plus a compactly supported piecewise smooth function. Integration by parts on either side gives
$$
 \widehat u(k)=\frac{d e^{-ika}}{(ik)^5}+O(|k|^{-6}).
$$
Thus for fixed $\kappa>0$ the part of (1) with $\kappa<k<\Lambda$ satisfies
$$
 \mu_{[\kappa,\Lambda]}(u)=C_a\log(\Lambda/\kappa)+O(1),\qquad
 C_a=\frac{A d^2}{\pi}
 =\frac{3G_N}{20\pi}(1-a)^2|G'_W(a)|^2. \tag{2}
$$
The error in the spectral integrand is $O(k^{-2})$, so $\mu_{[\Lambda,2\Lambda]}(u)\to C_a\log 2$. A finite-width detector can also be modeled by multiplying $\widehat u(k)$ by $\widehat\rho(\eta k)$ for a smooth, rapidly decreasing response kernel of unit integral. At fixed $\kappa>0$ its squared norm is $C_a\log(1/(\kappa\eta))+O(1)$ as $\eta\downarrow0$. The constant term depends on the response.

Changing the local primitive by a polynomial changes $u$ by a smooth compactly supported function. Its Fourier transform decreases faster than any power. Conversely, a full-line vector with finite (1) is locally in $H^{9/2}$ after fixing a distributional representative at zero frequency. Its high frequencies already satisfy that Sobolev bound, and its low frequencies give a smooth function locally. Any such representative solving the local equation would differ from $f_X$ by a smooth polynomial there. Equation (2) thus excludes a one-particle displacement representing this covector whenever $d\ne0$. The full Minkowski polarization preserves the radial quadrupole sector. A representative in the full one-particle space would give a bounded functional on that sector by Cauchy–Schwarz, subject to the same obstruction.

Other isolated fourth-derivative jumps at distinct positions cannot cancel the local divergence. In a finite collection their global cross terms have integrals proportional to $\int_\kappa^\Lambda \exp[ik(a_i-a_j)]\,dk/k$, which stay bounded for $a_i\ne a_j$. Contributions at the same position must first be summed with their signs. Localizing avoids imposing any condition on the continuation outside the test interval. An unlocalized primitive can have polynomial tails, so its behavior at zero frequency requires a separate prescription. Additional boundary degrees of freedom that change the local equation, or a different state assigning different UV weights, require a new calculation.

If $G'_W(a)=0$, the remaining fifth derivative of $u$ is piecewise smooth and $\widehat u=O(|k|^{-6})$, which is locally sufficient at this rim. For the compensated-packet reference, $G'_W(s)=(s-a)^5+O((s-a)^6)$. In this case the first jump occurs in $f_X^{(9)}$ and the tail above $\Lambda$ in (1) is $O(\Lambda^{-10})$. The classical packet pairing still tends to $-3/4$. The localized response therefore has finite one-particle norm even though its master profile has only eight continuous derivatives at the rim. ## Reproduce the finite-band test

Run `python3 -B checks/weak_fock.py` from the repository root. It uses $a=1/3$, $G_N=1$, and a compact reference with $G'_W(a)=1$. Its master profile is $16a(s-a)^5(1-y^2)^{12}/5!$ for $|y|<1$, zero outside, with $y=6(s-a)$. The taper has eleven continuous derivatives, sufficient for the leading asymptotic. For $k>0$ the code evaluates the original optical Hessian $H(k)$ on $g=e^{-ik(s-a)}$ with a smooth cutoff equal to one on the support of the reference. For the source extended by zero outside that support, $|\widehat f_X(k)|=|H(k)|/(A k^9)$ away from $k=0$. Consequently the integrand in logarithmic frequency is $|H(k)/k^4|^2/(\pi A)$. This computation integrates the optical slopes directly and tests the predicted jump coefficient without inserting the jump into the numerical integrand. [Executable check](../checks/weak_fock.py)

The measured weight divided by $\log 2$ falls from `0.029297769` on $[256,512]$ to `0.021246343` on $[4096,8192]$. The final line gives `Sharp rim coefficient 1/(15 pi) = 0.021220659`. The check repeats the highest band at twice the spatial quadrature density. Run `make check` for the full suite, which should exit successfully.
